Understanding Cloud Computing in Plain English

AC Avery Chen · June 18, 2026 · 7 min read

Renting computers instead of owning them

Strip away the marketing language, and cloud computing is a fairly simple idea: instead of buying, housing, and maintaining physical servers, you rent computing power, storage, and other infrastructure from a provider who already has it running at a large scale. What makes it useful isn't the concept alone — it's the flexibility. Capacity can be increased or decreased in minutes rather than weeks, and you generally pay only for what you actually use.

The terminology around cloud computing can make it sound more complicated than the underlying idea actually is. Stripping away the acronyms, most of what follows is really about answering one question: how much of the underlying machinery do you want to manage yourself, and how much are you comfortable handing to someone else in exchange for convenience.

The three common layers

Cloud services are usually described in three layers, each handing over a different amount of control in exchange for a different amount of convenience:

  • Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS). Raw virtual machines, storage, and networking — you manage the operating system and everything above it.
  • Platform as a Service (PaaS). A managed environment for running applications, where the provider handles the underlying servers and you focus on code.
  • Software as a Service (SaaS). A finished application delivered over the internet, with no infrastructure to manage at all.

Most organizations use a mix of all three, depending on the task — raw infrastructure for a custom application, a platform service for a quick internal tool, and off-the-shelf software for email or accounting.

None of the three layers is inherently better than the others — the right choice depends entirely on how much you need to customize versus how quickly you need something running. A team building a novel product usually needs more control than a team just needing standard email and file storage.

What actually changes when you move to the cloud

The most meaningful shift isn't technical — it's financial and operational. Large upfront hardware costs become ongoing operating costs. Scaling for a traffic spike becomes a configuration change instead of a hardware purchase. The tradeoff is a dependency on the provider's reliability and pricing, and a need to understand the shared responsibility model: the provider secures the underlying infrastructure, but configuration, access control, and data handling are still the customer's job.

A common mistake

Treating the cloud as a place to just lift and shift an existing setup, unchanged, tends to produce disappointing results. The real benefits show up when systems are designed to take advantage of elasticity — scaling components independently, using managed databases instead of self-hosting, and automating what used to be manual server maintenance.

Getting started without overcomplicating it

Teams new to cloud infrastructure often over-engineer their first setup, reaching for complex, highly configurable services before they've outgrown something simpler. A reasonable starting point is a managed platform service for the application itself, paired with a managed database, and only moving to raw infrastructure once there's a specific, demonstrated need for that level of control.

Cost visibility is worth setting up early too. Cloud billing can grow quietly if unused resources are left running, so a basic habit of reviewing what's actually provisioned each month — and shutting down anything idle — prevents the kind of surprise bill that gives the cloud a reputation for being unpredictable, when in practice the unpredictability usually comes from a lack of oversight rather than the technology itself.

Frequently asked questions

Is the cloud always cheaper than owning hardware?

Not always. At a very large, steady scale, owning hardware can sometimes be cheaper. The cloud tends to win when demand is variable or unpredictable.

What does 'multi-cloud' mean?

Using more than one cloud provider, often to avoid depending entirely on a single vendor or to take advantage of specific services each one offers.

Is my data safe in the cloud?

It can be, but safety depends on configuration — access controls, encryption, and permissions are the customer's responsibility, not just the provider's.
