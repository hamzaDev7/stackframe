A Beginner's Guide to Understanding Large Language Models

AC Avery Chen · July 25, 2026 · 8 min read

Prediction, not understanding

At its core, a large language model is trained to do one thing extremely well: predict the next piece of text given everything that came before it. That sounds almost too simple to explain the abilities these systems display, but scale changes what a simple task can do. Trained on enormous amounts of text and tuned with a huge number of internal parameters, next-word prediction becomes capable of drafting, summarizing, translating, and reasoning through problems it was never explicitly programmed to solve.

It's worth being precise about what this means. The model isn't consulting a database of facts or reasoning the way a person does. It's generating text that is statistically consistent with patterns it observed during training — which is why it can be fluent and confident while still being wrong.

This distinction between prediction and understanding isn't a minor technicality — it explains both why these systems are so broadly useful and why they fail in ways that can catch people off guard. Keeping it in mind while reading the rest of this guide makes the later sections on limitations much less surprising.

How training actually works, roughly

  • Pretraining. The model reads a very large amount of text and adjusts its internal parameters to get better at predicting the next word, over and over, across billions of examples.
  • Fine-tuning. The base model is further trained on more specific, curated examples — often including human feedback — to make its responses more helpful and appropriate.
  • Inference. When you actually use the model, none of that training happens again. It's applying what it already learned to generate a response to your specific input.

It also helps to know that different tools handle these tradeoffs differently — some are tuned to hedge more, others to answer more confidently even at the cost of occasional errors. Paying attention to how a specific tool behaves, rather than assuming all language models behave identically, makes it easier to calibrate how much to trust a given answer.

Where the limits show up

Because a language model generates plausible text rather than verified facts, it can produce confident-sounding statements that are simply incorrect — often called hallucination. It also has no persistent memory beyond what's included in a given conversation, no built-in way to know if a fact has changed since training, and no genuine understanding of consequences, even when its output reads as if it does.

A useful mental model

Treating a language model less like an oracle and more like a very well-read collaborator — fast, broadly knowledgeable, occasionally confidently wrong — tends to produce better outcomes than trusting its output uncritically.

Using these tools well

Getting good results from a language model usually comes down to how the task is framed. Specific, well-scoped requests with relevant context produce noticeably better output than vague ones, largely because the model has less room to guess at what's actually needed. Asking it to show its reasoning, or to flag uncertainty rather than guessing silently, also tends to surface mistakes before they matter.

For anything where being wrong has real consequences — a factual claim, a piece of code going into production, a decision affecting someone else — treating the model's output as a draft to verify, not a finished answer, remains the safest default, regardless of how confident the response sounds.

Frequently asked questions

Do language models understand what they're saying?

Not in the way people do. They generate statistically likely text based on patterns in training data, without an internal model of meaning or truth.

Why do models sometimes make things up?

Because they're optimized to produce plausible-sounding text, not to verify facts. When the model lacks reliable information, it can still generate a confident, incorrect answer.

Can a language model learn from a single conversation?

Not permanently. Its underlying knowledge stays fixed after training; it only 'remembers' what's included in the current conversation.
